How you train a model when you can only score it

No prior reinforcement learning knowledge needed. Every term is defined where it first appears. You'll build the real training algorithm from scratch in plain numpy, learn why writing the scoring function is the part that actually matters, and then train a model for real.

Start with the problem

Normally you train a model by showing it the right answer: "when you see this, produce that." But often you don't have the right answer written out. You only have a way to check an answer once you see one.

You want a model that reasons well. You don't have a file of ideal reasoning. What you do have is a checker: did the code run? is the number 391? So your situation is:

  • You cannot say "produce this exact text."
  • You can say "produce something, and I'll tell you if it was good."

That's the whole setup. Now the obstacle, which is worth being precise about. Training normally means computing a gradient — the direction to nudge each of the model's internal numbers to make your score go up. That needs an unbroken chain of smooth math from "internal number" to "score." Trace it: 

internal numbers → probabilities → PICK A WORD → text → score
     (smooth)          (smooth)        ← breaks here

Everything is smooth until you pick a word. Picking one option out of many is a jump, not a slope — there's no gentle incline through "I chose word 4 instead of word 3." The chain breaks, and you can't differentiate your way to a better model.

The trick

You can't compute the gradient. But you can estimate it by trying things.

  1. Have the model produce some output.
  2. Score it.
  3. Good score? Adjust the model to make that output more likely.
  4. Bad score? Make it less likely.
  5. Repeat a few thousand times.

That's the entire method. It has a name — policy gradient — but the name matters less than what it does: it turns "I can score things" into "I can improve things," without ever needing to know what the ideal output was.

The one problem with this

If all your scores are positive, every output you sample gets pushed up — including bad ones, just less hard. You're reinforcing bad answers.

The fix is one subtraction: use the score minus the average score. Now anything below average gets a negative number, which pushes it down. That subtracted number is called the baseline, and the result — how much better than typical this output was — is called the advantage.

And it turns out subtracting a baseline costs you nothing at all. It's not a trade-off; it's free. Module 01 has you measure exactly how much noise it removes, and the answer is larger than you'd guess.

Where does the average come from?

You need "the average score for this question." How would you know it?

The old answer: train a second neural network whose only job is to look at a question and predict "you'll probably score about 0.44 here." That's what the older algorithm PPO does. It costs you an entire extra model, and early on its guesses are bad.

The modern answer is better, and slightly embarrassing once you see it:

Don't predict the average. Just ask the same question several times and measure it.

Ask 8 times, get 8 answers, score all 8. Their average is the average score for that question. No second network, no prediction. You just looked.

That's GRPO. A batch of answers to the same question is a group, and each answer's advantage is measured against its own group. "Group Relative Policy Optimization" is a complete description of the algorithm.

Then the hard part: writing the score

Once you can train on a score, notice what's left wide open. Everything now depends on that score. The algorithm has no opinion about what's good — it faithfully maximizes whatever number you hand it.

The lesson that costs people GPU-days

Module 02 hands you a scoring function that reads perfectly sensibly: "did the right answer appear anywhere in the reply?" Versions of it ship in real codebases.

It gives full marks to a reply that says the answer is not 391, to one that guesses five numbers at once, and to one that simply lists every number from 380 to 400. It scores 5 out of 12 against an adversarial set.

That's worse than it sounds. A noisy score is survivable — random errors average out. An exploitable one doesn't, because training is a search process whose entire job is finding the highest score available. Listing numbers really does score better than multiplying, and it's much easier. The model isn't cheating; it's doing exactly what you asked.

Self-check

Try to answer before expanding. If you can't, that's the thing to go reread.

Why can't we train the model the ordinary way — just showing it the right answer?

Because for a lot of what we want, we don't have the right answer written out. Nobody has a file of ideal reasoning traces. What we have instead is a checker: run the code and see if it passes, or compare the final number to 391.

So we can't say "produce this text." We can only say "produce something, and I'll tell you how good it was." Everything else follows from that limitation.

Where exactly does the math "break" so you can't compute a gradient?

At the moment the model picks a word. Everything up to that point is smooth — internal numbers flow into probabilities continuously. But picking one option out of thousands is a discrete jump. There's no gentle slope through "I chose word 4 rather than word 3," so there's nothing to follow.

That single break is why this whole field needs a different approach.

In one sentence, what does the training method actually do?

Sample some outputs, and make the ones that scored well more likely.

That's genuinely it. Karpathy's framing is worth keeping: it's ordinary supervised learning, where the training examples are the model's own samples, weighted by how well each one did. If your one-sentence version has more clauses than that, you probably haven't simplified it enough yet.

What goes wrong if all your scores are positive?

Every output you sample gets pushed up — including the bad ones, just less hard. You're actively reinforcing mediocre answers. It still works eventually, because good answers get bigger pushes, but you're making the model dig the signal out of a pile of noise it never needed to see.

The fix is one subtraction: use the score minus the average score. Now below-average outputs get a negative multiplier and are pushed down.

You add +10 to every score. The best possible model doesn't change. Why does learning get 359× noisier?

Without a baseline, the size of every update is proportional to the score. Make every score about 10 and every update becomes a big shove toward whatever was sampled — while the part you actually care about, the 0-to-1 spread between good and bad, is a small ripple on top. Those big shoves cancel out on average, but not in any batch of finite size.

The practical version: if you write a scoring function giving 10 points for "replied in valid JSON" and 1 point for "the answer is correct," you've built that exact problem into your rewards. Keep your scoring components on comparable scales.

GRPO needs the average score for a question. Where does it get it?

It asks the question several times and averages what comes back. That's the whole idea.

The older approach (PPO) trains an entire second neural network to predict that average. GRPO points out that you don't have to predict something you can simply measure — and you can measure it here because asking a model the same question 8 times is cheap. That's a luxury you wouldn't have if each attempt meant physically resetting a robot.

Asking each question only once didn't learn slowly — it didn't learn at all. Why?

Because the advantage is "your score minus the group's average," and the average of one number is that same number. So the advantage is always exactly zero, every update is zero, and the model never moves.

This is the sharpest way to see where the signal actually comes from: not from scores being high, only from scores being different within a group.

A question where all 8 answers score 1.0, and one where all 8 score 0.0. What do they have in common?

Both produce exactly zero learning. No spread means no advantage, regardless of whether the shared score was high or low. Too easy and too hard are the same failure, and both burn compute for nothing.

Which tells you how to pick training questions: you want ones the model gets right sometimes. That's most of what "curriculum design" means in practice.

Why is an exploitable scoring function much worse than a merely noisy one?

Noise averages out across thousands of examples. An exploit doesn't — it gets amplified, because training is a search process and its entire job is finding the highest score available.

Trace it: one answer stumbles onto "list every number from 380 to 400" and scores 1.0 while the careful answers score 0.0 on an arithmetic slip. That gives the trick a positive advantage, so it's reinforced, so it's sampled more, so it wins more groups. A few hundred steps later the model has abandoned arithmetic — and the score curve looked like a triumph the whole time, because the score is the thing being hacked.

Why is a formatting reward specifically dangerous to weight heavily?

Two properties make any scoring component a magnet for the model's attention: it's cheap (any model can satisfy it immediately, no skill required) and it's reliable (it pays out every single time). Correctness is neither.

So early in training, when the model is still bad at the actual task, formatting is often the only component reliably producing score differences within a group — which means it dominates exactly when the model is most impressionable. The rule: an extra reward should be a tiebreaker, never a target.

Your fixed scoring function passes all 12 attacks. Is it safe?

No — it's safe against the attacks someone thought of, which is a much weaker claim. Still open: a lucky guess scores full marks (we reward outcomes, not reasoning); the answer leaking into the prompt somewhere; and equivalent answers written differently, where 0.5 and 1/2 score differently.

Loosening the comparison to fix those reopens the door to the attacks you just closed. That trade-off never fully goes away. The real lesson: you cannot verify a scoring function by reading it — everyone's looks fine. You verify it by trying to break it.

Glossary

The terms worth having straight. The repo's GLOSSARY.md has the full list, including the infrastructure vocabulary you'll need later.

Logits
The raw scores a model produces, one per possible next word, before they're turned into probabilities. Any number, positive or negative. [2.0, 1.0, -1.0]
Softmax
The function that turns logits into probabilities — exponentiate each, then divide by the total so they sum to 1. It stretches the gaps. [2.0, 1.0, -1.0] → [0.705, 0.259, 0.035]
Policy
The model's rule for choosing what to produce. When you read "the policy improved," it just means the model got better.
Rollout
One run of the model on one question, producing one answer. Also called a completion or a sample.
Reward
A number saying how good one answer was. You write the function that produces it. There's no deeper truth to it, which is exactly why it's hard.
Gradient
The direction you'd nudge a set of numbers to make some output increase fastest. Itself a list of numbers.
Baseline
A number subtracted from the score before using it, so below-average answers get pushed down rather than weakly up. Usually the average score.
Advantage
Score minus baseline — how much better than typical this answer was. This is the number that actually drives learning.
Group
Several answers to the same question. Ask once, get 8 answers, that's a group of 8.
GRPO
Group Relative Policy Optimization. Measure each answer against the average of its own group, instead of training a second network to predict that average. The name is a complete description.
Value network
The second network the older method (PPO) trains just to predict the baseline. GRPO's whole contribution is not needing one.
Variance
How much something jumps around between samples. High variance means you need many samples before an average settles down.
Environment
In this stack: a set of questions, a way to find the answer in a reply, and a scoring function. Bundled together. No simulator involved.
Rubric
Several scoring functions combined with weights, so you can say "correctness is worth five times as much as formatting."
Reward hacking
When the model scores highly without doing the task you wanted. Not a bug in the model — a bug in your scoring function.
Bandit
The simplest setting: several slot machines, pull one, get a payout, and your choice doesn't change anything about the next round. Close to how single-question model training actually behaves.

Then the loop: read the module's README.md, fill in the TODOs, run verify.py, compare against the answer key in solutions/. 

uv run python modules/01-rl-foundations/exercise_1_bandit.py

The graders check behaviour, not exact code — there's more than one right way to write most of these. When something's wrong they name what broke and show what they saw, rather than just failing.

Each module also has a WORKED_EXAMPLES.md with the arithmetic done out in full, including a numerical check that proves the update formula is right instead of asking you to take it on faith.

One environment note

The pyenv Python 3.12 on this machine was built without _lzma, which breaks the HuggingFace datasets library. pyproject.toml pins uv's own Python build to avoid it. If you ever see a mysterious ModuleNotFoundError: _lzma, that's the cause.
